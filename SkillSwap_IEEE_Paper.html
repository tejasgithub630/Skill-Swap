Abstract—Peer skill-exchange platforms let learners teach what they know in return for learning what they need. The main issue is that a direct swap requires two users who each want exactly what the other person offers. In smaller groups, this strict reciprocity rule leaves a lot of learners completely unmatched. Furthermore, because users self-declare their skills and often lack reputation data when they join, the quality of these matches can be pretty weak. To solve this, we built SkillSwap, a full-stack platform (React 19, Node.js/Express 5, MySQL) that addresses these issues. It relies on a hybrid skill-compatibility score built over a curated taxonomy, assessment-verified credentials graded by a server-side proctoring pipeline, a Bayesian trust model driven by feedback, and a three-way "learning ring" search that closes exchange cycles (A→B→C→A) using per-hop session credits. We evaluated the matching engine in a reproducible simulation using its 15-skill taxonomy. In sparse groups where every user offers and seeks a single skill, finding three-way rings increased the share of matched users by up to 13.5 percentage points (jumping from 67.0% to 80.5% for 30 users). We also found that ranking tutors using our feedback-driven trust model raised the actual quality of assigned tutors from 3.77 to 4.42 out of 5—closing 82% of the gap to a perfect oracle. Using per-hop settlement also ensured that we didn't leave 30% of delivered sessions uncredited, which would have happened if we required the entire ring to complete before paying out. We also quantified a few limitations: the lexical similarity rule accidentally admitted 22.7% of unrelated same-domain skill pairs, our deployed cold-start weight didn't change session inequality at all (Gini 0.855 vs. 0.856), and the ring discovery process unfortunately requires a quadratic number of relationship lookups.

Index Terms—peer learning, skill exchange, barter matching, cycle detection, trust model, cold start, online assessment.

I. INTRODUCTION

When learners of similar standing help each other out, it usually improves understanding and engagement for both the person explaining the concept and the person listening [8]. However, almost every online learning platform today is still organized around one-way delivery of recorded courses. Skill-exchange platforms flip this idea on its head: every user acts as both a teacher for skills they already possess and a learner for skills they lack, and sessions are paid for with teaching time instead of actual money.

The biggest obstacle to this is reciprocity. A direct swap requires a double coincidence of wants: user A must want what B offers, and B has to want what A offers. Traditional barter markets face this exact same structural problem. In things like kidney-exchange programs, researchers proved that allowing trades among three or more parties substantially increases the number of people who get matched [9]. However, learning environments introduce two specific problems of their own. First, users self-declare their skills, so the matching algorithm might pair a learner with someone who doesn't actually know how to teach the topic. Second, brand new members don't have any feedback history, which means ranking people by reputation alone just funnels all the sessions to a few popular veterans [10].

Most existing educational recommenders just push content, courses, or career paths [1]–[5], and trust-aware hybrid systems try to improve those recommendations by adding reputation signals [6]. But none of these systems actually evaluate multi-party exchanges between learners. This paper introduces SkillSwap, a fully deployed platform that handles reciprocity, skill verification, and trust all at once, and we provide a reproducible evaluation of its matching engine.

Our core contributions are:

1) System. We designed and implemented a three-tier skill-exchange platform that includes a canonical skill taxonomy, a 470-question assessment bank covering 23 topics, server-side grading, browser proctoring, and publicly verifiable certificates (Sections III–VI).

2) Algorithms. We formally describe our matching pipeline, which features a hybrid skill-pair score, a composite match score, a Bayesian trust model updated by session feedback, a cold-start exploration boost, and a three-way learning-ring search that uses a ring-health score and per-hop session credits (Section IV).

3) Evaluation. We ran a seeded simulation that directly calls the production matching module without any modifications to measure edge validity, exchange coverage, ranking quality, the feedback loop, fairness, ring completion, and system scalability (Section VII).

4) Analysis. We provide a quantified discussion of where the current design falls short, along with concrete parameter guidance for the next iteration (Section VIII).

II. RELATED WORK

A. Educational recommender systems

Kaiss et al. combined collaborative and content-based filtering to personalize learning resources [1], and Amin et al. built a MOOC recommender tailored for IoT-enabled smart education [2]. Tamilarasi et al. focused on extracting skills from profiles to generate feedback-oriented recommendations [3], while Selvaraj et al. and Kadu et al. mapped specific skills to career roles to predict placement outcomes [4], [5]. Thaoroijam et al. proved that integrating trust into hybrid recommenders improves overall performance [6]. The limitation here is that these systems recommend static items to a learner; SkillSwap is built to match actual people to each other under a strict reciprocity constraint.

B. Matching markets and barter exchange

Classic two-sided matching is usually solved with stable matching algorithms [9], but that assumes you have two completely disjoint groups. Skill exchange is one-sided because every user is both offering and wanting something at the same time. Kidney exchange is probably the closest real-world analogue. Roth et al. formalized the concept of exchange cycles [9], Abraham et al. proved that capping the cycle length (usually at three) keeps the matching process tractable while still recovering most of the benefits [9], and Dickerson et al. figured out how to make those allocations robust against individual exchanges failing [9]. You can enumerate the elementary cycles of a directed graph exactly [9]; SkillSwap takes advantage of this by running a bounded depth-three search originating from the requesting user.

C. Reputation, trust and cold start

Reputation systems gather user feedback to predict how someone will behave in the future [8]. Bayesian formulations—like the beta reputation system—are great because they temper scores that are based on very few ratings [8]. Pure reputation ranking suffers heavily from the cold-start problem [10] and popularity bias, where already-popular providers just get more and more exposure [10]. To ensure quality, automated online proctoring has been heavily studied for unsupervised exams [7]; SkillSwap applies a lightweight, browser-side version of this to gate skill verification.

III. SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE

SkillSwap operates on a standard three-tier architecture (Figure 1: Three-tier architecture). The presentation tier is a React 19 single-page application built with Vite; it gives users role-specific dashboards for engineering and medical learners, an admin console, the proctored assessment interface, the learning-ring view, and a public certificate verifier. The application tier runs on a Node.js/Express 5 REST API organized into four main functional modules: the matching engine, the assessment service, trust and feedback, and the skill taxonomy. Every protected route sits behind JWT authentication middleware (where tokens expire after seven days), and the administrator routes require a specific admin role. Passwords are all hashed using bcrypt. Finally, the data tier relies on a relational MySQL database accessed via the Sequelize ORM using foreign-key associations.

Skill taxonomy. If you rely on free-text skill names, you break the matching engine ("ReactJS", "React.js", "react"). SkillSwap fixes this by storing every skill once as a canonical record featuring a slug, a category, a domain, and any number of aliases. Users can propose a new skill via a request that an admin can either approve, reject, or put under review. Typed, weighted relationships (like RELATED, COMPLEMENTARY, SPECIALIZATION) tie these canonical skills together. The taxonomy we seeded the database with contains 15 skills (9 engineering, 6 medical) and 8 relationships with strengths ranging from 0.80 to 0.95 (for instance, TypeScript→React is 0.95 and Radiology→Cardiology is 0.85).

User skills. Whenever a user adds a teachable skill, it gets stored alongside a proficiency level (beginner to expert), their years of experience, and a verification status (self-declared, verified, expired, or revoked). Wanted skills are simply stored with a priority ranking. User profiles also hold the aggregated feedback needed for the trust model: their mean rating, total rating count, attendance reliability, total sessions conducted, and a balance of escrow credits.

IV. MATCHING ALGORITHMS

Let's assume a mentor offers a skill (\(o\)) and a learner wants a skill (\(w\)). All of our scores sit between 0 and 1; we use a clip function to bound values to that interval. The parameter values we discuss here reflect the actual deployed engine (Table II: Weights and Thresholds).

A. Hybrid skill-pair compatibility

Our pair score blends together lexical, taxonomic, and contextual evidence:

$$ s(o,w) = \text{clip}\big( m_p \cdot (0.45 \sigma + 0.25 \tau + 0.15 \delta + 0.15 c_v) \big) \quad (1) $$

Here, \(\sigma\) is 1 if they are the exact same canonical skill. Otherwise, it's the character-bigram Jaccard similarity of the normalized names [3], which gets bumped up to at least 0.75 if one name contains the other as a substring. \(\tau\) represents the relationship strength between the two skills in either direction (or 0 if they are unrelated). \(\delta\) is 1 if both skills share a domain and 0.5 otherwise. \(c_v\) is 1.25 for an assessment-verified offer and 0.85 for a self-declared one. Finally, \(m_p\) is a multiplier from {0.85, 1.00, 1.10, 1.20} that scales based on whether the user is a beginner or an expert.

B. Mentor experience, trust and feedback

We saturate teaching experience logarithmically so that veterans can't just accumulate an unbeatable, unbounded advantage:

$$ E = 0.5 \min\left(1, \frac{\ln(1+n)}{\ln 26}\right) + 0.35\pi + 0.15 \min\left(1, \frac{y}{5}\right) \quad (2) $$

In this formula, \(n\) is the number of sessions they've conducted, \(\pi\) is the highest proficiency weight among all of the mentor's teaching skills, and \(y\) is their years of experience.

Learner feedback is processed through a Bayesian average rating that intentionally shrinks sparse evidence toward a prior baseline [8]:

$$ \bar{R} = \frac{C m + k \bar{r}}{C + k}, \quad C = 3, \; m = 4.5 \quad (3) $$
$$ T = 0.55 \frac{\bar{R}}{5} + 0.45 \rho \quad (4) $$

where \(\bar{r}\) is the mean of \(k\) session ratings and \(\rho\) (between 0 and 1) represents the mentor's attendance reliability. Both are stored directly on the mentor's profile. Updating \(k\), \(\bar{r}\), and \(\rho\) after each rated session closes a feedback loop in which past learners' experiences shape future rankings.

C. Cold-start exploration

To make sure newly certified mentors actually get some exposure, we apply a temporary boost that decays linearly over their first five sessions, but only if at least one of their teaching skills is actually assessment-verified:

$$ b = \begin{cases} 0.25 \left(1 - \frac{n}{5}\right) & \text{if } n < 5 \text{ and verified} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \quad (5) $$

If a learner is facing a tight deadline and activates urgent mode, this boost gets suppressed, and the weights shift entirely toward trust and experience.

D. Composite direct-match score

For a learner A and a candidate mentor B, let \(S_{AB}\) be the highest pair score of any offer from A against any want from B, and \(S_{BA}\) be the converse. The final match score combines this skill fit with the mentor's quality signals and a reciprocity term:

$$ M = w_s \max(S_{AB}, S_{BA}) + w_t T_B + w_e E_B + w_c b_B + 0.1 Q \quad (6) $$

where \(Q = 1\) if both \(S_{AB}\) and \(S_{BA}\) exceed 0.5 (meaning it's a genuine two-way swap) and \(Q = (S_{AB} + S_{BA})/2\) otherwise. We clip this score to [0, 1] and display it to the learner as a percentage, alongside a textual breakdown explaining the individual components (Algorithm 1).

E. Three-way learning rings

When we can't find a reciprocal partner, SkillSwap triggers a search for a learning ring where A teaches B, B teaches C, and C teaches A (Figure 2: Three-way learning ring). It constructs a directed graph where an edge u→v exists as long as the best pair score of a u-offer against a v-want hits at least 0.40, with an edge weight of:

$$ \omega_{uv} = 0.45 s_{uv} + 0.25 T_u + 0.20 E_u + 0.10 b_u \quad (7) $$

It then enumerates every directed triangle starting from the requesting user (Algorithm 2). Because a ring instantly fails if any single participant flakes out, its health score takes the geometric mean of the three edge weights and multiplies it by the weakest participant's attendance reliability [9]:

$$ H = 100 (\omega_{AB} \omega_{BC} \omega_{CA})^{1/3} \cdot \min(\rho_A, \rho_B, \rho_C) \quad (8) $$

Rings with an \(H\) score ≥ 45 are returned to the user, sorted by \(H\). Because synchronizing three people's schedules is notoriously difficult, rings actually run asynchronously. A mentor claims their session credit (stored as an "escrow credit") the moment their specific hop is completed, rather than waiting for the entire ring to close. Every user starts with five credits.

Edge construction requires evaluating every ordered pair of users, meaning it costs O(N²·|o|·|w|) pair scores for N users with |o| offered and |w| wanted skills. Triangle enumeration runs in O(d³) for an out-degree of d, because the closing edge C→A is found by a linear scan.

V. ASSESSMENT, FEEDBACK AND CREDENTIALS

A skill only becomes officially verified through our assessment pipeline. When a learner starts a test, the server generates an attempt record and sends over the questions without the correct answers attached. The browser immediately enforces full-screen mode, disables copy/paste and keyboard shortcuts, and turns on a webcam preview. If a user leaves full-screen or switches tabs, the browser ends the attempt and reports the event to the server, which independently invalidates the test if it logs six violations.

Once submitted, the server checks the elapsed time against the limit (plus a 60-second grace period) and grades every answer itself, storing each answer with its correctness.

Learners get immediate feedback: a per-question review marking each answer correct or incorrect, and a breakdown of correct answers by difficulty (easy, medium, hard). A score of at least 80% passes. We derive proficiency directly from the score (≥90% expert, ≥80% advanced, ≥70% intermediate). Passing generates a unique certificate of the form SKILLSWAP-<SKILL>-<YEAR>-<6 digits>, valid for 24 months. The user's skill is marked verified, which raises \(c_v\) in (1) and unlocks the cold-start boost. Anyone can check a certificate's status at a public URL; administrators can also revoke certificates. Table III (Assessment and Credential Parameters) summarizes the parameters.

VI. IMPLEMENTATION

The client relies on React 19.2 with React Router 7 and Lucide icons, all built via Vite 8. The production bundle comes in at 813 kB of JavaScript (242 kB when gzip-compressed). The server runs Express 5.2 and Sequelize 6.37 with the mysql2 driver, jsonwebtoken 9, and bcrypt 6. Across both the client and server, the codebase is roughly 17,500 lines of JavaScript/JSX, including 2,238 lines of course data and 873 lines of question banks. The REST API offers 42 endpoints spread across ten route modules, plus a health check. A seeding script creates the taxonomy, assessments, and demonstration accounts; a separate migration adds the trust and escrow fields to profiles.

VII. EXPERIMENTAL EVALUATION

A. Setup

Harness. To run our evaluation, we imported the production matching module perfectly intact. The only change was swapping out its database access for an in-memory store loaded with the same relationship rows our seeding script inserts. We tracked every relationship lookup because, in a real deployment, each of those is a database round trip.

Populations. We drew synthetic users across our real 15-skill taxonomy (60% engineering, 40% medical). Offered and wanted skills followed a Zipf(0.8) popularity curve with different orderings for supply and demand. Proficiency was distributed uniformly over four levels, 40% of offered skills were verified, latent teaching quality was uniform on [2.5, 5], and attendance reliability was uniform on [0.6, 1.0]. Each configuration is repeated with 20 seeds and tables report means.

Ground truth. An offer genuinely satisfies a want only if it is the exact same canonical skill or if the two skills are linked in the taxonomy. This independent criterion lets us measure how often the engine's scores actually align with real skill relevance.

B. Validity of skill-pair edges

We evaluated (1) exhaustively against every ordered pair of the 15 skills across all proficiencies and verification statuses (Table IV: Skill-Pair Scores). Every legitimately taxonomy-related pair cleared the 0.40 edge threshold (minimum score 0.406), meaning our recall for genuine edges was a perfect 100%. However, 22.7% of completely unrelated pairs within the same domain also cleared the hurdle. This happens for two reasons. First, the verification and domain variables contribute up to 0.34 independently of actual skill relevance (0.41 after the expert multiplier), so an expert, verified mentor ends up connecting to every single skill in their domain. Second, our bigram overlap check accidentally rewards lexical look-alikes: Java→JavaScript scores 0.61 because of the substring rule, and Cardiology→Pathology gets 0.42 just because they share the "ology" suffix.

C. Exchange coverage: two-way versus three-way

Coverage is simply the fraction of users who end up with at least one feasible exchange built entirely from genuine edges. As shown in Figure 3 (Share of users with a feasible exchange) and Table V (Coverage by Market Density), three-way rings are highly effective when reciprocity is scarce. If every user offers and wants exactly one skill, rings boost coverage by 11.3, 13.5, and 12.8 percentage points at 20, 30, and 50 users. But as the market thickens, the gain shrinks. If users offer two skills, the boost peaks at 9.5 points, and if they offer three and want two, direct swaps alone cover 96.5% of users even in a tiny 10-user group.

The sheer volume of rings also blows up: in the default population, the engine returns on average 147, 620, and 2,464 rings per user at 50, 100, and 200 users respectively.

D. Direct-match ranking

For populations scaling from 50 to 400 users, we ranked all candidates for 25 sampled learners. On average, 0.69–0.76 of the top-5 candidates turned out to be genuine reciprocal partners, compared to just 0.18–0.20 if we used a random order. The score separated reciprocal pairs from pairs with zero skill fit almost perfectly (ROC AUC 0.998–0.999). However, the absolute percentage isn't calibrated perfectly: pairs with absolutely no skill relationship still pulled a mean score of 49.3% (range 34–80%), because the trust, experience, and reciprocity terms contribute regardless of skill fit. Genuine reciprocal pairs averaged 80.7%.

E. Feedback-driven trust

We measured how well our trust score ranks 200 tutors where we already knew their latent quality (adding Gaussian noise with σ = 0.8 to the ratings). Table VI (Ranking Tutors by Trust) shows that both the Bayesian and raw scores track latent quality closely (Spearman ρ of 0.81 and 0.82 at five ratings). The Bayesian prior improves identification of the top 20 tutors (precision 0.47 vs. 0.41), but it only moderately protects against a single lucky rating: a weak newcomer (quality 3.0) with one five-star rating is still ranked above 93% of tutors.

F. Cold-start fairness

We tracked thirty mentors all teaching the exact same skill: six veterans (15–30 sessions, ratings 4.5–4.9) and 24 newcomers (0–2 sessions, 70% verified). Over 400 requests, we measured the Gini coefficient of the delivered sessions and tracked how many newcomers managed to get at least one booking (Table VII: Effect of Cold-Start, and Figure 5: Newcomer mentors). At our deployed weight of 0.15, the boost barely makes a dent in inequality: the Gini sits at 0.855, and newcomers only capture 3.2% of the sessions. If we raise the cold-start weight \(w_c\) to 0.60, we manage to get 72% of newcomers their first teaching gig and pull the Gini down to 0.803, all at a completely negligible cost to overall delivered quality (4.146 vs. 4.150).

G. Ring health and credit settlement

We generated all distinct rings returned by the engine across 20 sparse cohorts of 30 users and simulated each ring 20 times. Mentors attended their hop based on their latent reliability probability. Overall, 58.6% of rings completed all three hops. Ring completion does rise alongside the health score (Table VIII: Ring Completion), but the score itself is a fairly weak predictor (AUC 0.57) because 95% of the rings fall into the lowest health band. Settlement logic matters significantly more. Because we issue per-hop settlement credits for every delivered session, mentors get paid when they do their part. If we used an all-or-nothing settlement where credits only payout when the full ring completes, mentors in broken rings would go unpaid, leaving 30.0% of the 97,309 simulated delivered sessions totally uncredited.

H. Scalability

Direct ranking scales linearly, growing from 214 lookups at 25 users to 3,720 at 400. Ring discovery, however, forces the engine to rebuild every edge on every single call, which grows quadratically (Figure 6: Scalability lookups). At 400 users, it takes 874,107 lookups (about 1.88 seconds even in memory). If we run that against a real database, every lookup adds a network round trip, creating a massive bottleneck.

VIII. DISCUSSION

When do rings help? Three-way rings shine in small cohorts where learners have highly narrow needs, like a specialized medical batch. In thick markets, they just add compute overhead without providing much extra coverage.

Edge precision. The 0.40 threshold admits far too many unrelated pairs. We need to gate the contextual terms (domain, verification, proficiency) behind a non-zero taxonomic signal and replace the lexical substring rule with strict alias-based normalization.

Fairness and trust. Based on the data, the cold-start weight should absolutely be raised to around 0.6 to help newcomers without sacrificing quality. A stronger Bayesian prior (larger C) would also better resist single-rating manipulation.

Scalability. Loading the relationship table just once per request and caching edges between requests would eliminate almost all of those lookups.

Limitations and threats to validity. We ran these evaluations using simulated users mapped over a 15-skill taxonomy, meaning the absolute numbers depend entirely on our population model. Latent quality and reliability are modelled rather than observed, and we haven't conducted a real user study yet. Also, a few parts of the evaluated build are still incomplete: the trust model reads stored aggregates, but the API doesn't yet formally record post-session attendance, and session credits aren't firmly locked to confirmed ring hops.

IX. CONCLUSION

SkillSwap successfully transforms one-way online learning into a reciprocal peer exchange. By layering verified credentials, feedback-driven trust, and three-way learning rings with per-hop session credits, we solve the major structural issues of skill bartering. A reproducible evaluation proves that rings boost coverage by 13.5 points in sparse cohorts, the feedback loop captures 82% of achievable quality gains, and per-hop settlement prevents 30% of delivered sessions from going uncredited. In future iterations, we plan to fix the edge precision issues, calibrate the scores, and implement post-session rating captures with two-party confirmation for a full-semester user study.
